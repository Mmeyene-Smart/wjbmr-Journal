ABSTRACT

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is characterized by frequent electrolyte imbalances, which can exacerbate disease complications, particularly during vaso-occlusive crises (VOC). These imbalances, coupled with oxidative stress due to reduced antioxidant vitamins, contribute significantly to SCD patho-physiology. This study aimed to investigate the serum levels of key electrolytes—calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus—as well as antioxidant vitamins A, C, and E in SCD patients during crises, those in steady state and healthy controls in Kano, Nigeria. A comparative analysis was conducted on serum samples from SCD patients in crisis 30(37.5%), those in steady state 30(37.5%), and healthy controls 20 (25%). Serum calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus levels were measured spectrophotometrically, along with antioxidant vitamins. Statistical comparisons were made using ANOVA, and Pearson correlation was employed to analyze relationships between electrolyte levels. The SCD patients in crisis and steady state exhibited significantly lower serum calcium (p=0.035) and magnesium (p=0.004) levels, and higher phosphate ((p=0.016) compared to healthy controls. The study also found significantly reduced levels of antioxidant vitamins A, C, and E in SCD patients, particularly during crises (p<0.05). Correlation analysis revealed strong positive relationships between electrolyte levels, suggesting parallel physiological changes in SCD. The findings highlighted significant disruptions in electrolyte and antioxidant vitamin levels during VOC in SCD patients. These imbalances may contribute to the disease's severity and progression. Dietary antioxidants may offer therapeutic benefits in restoring red-ox balance and mitigating oxidative stress in SCD.

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a severe genetic disorder, particularly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, caused by a mutation in the β globin gene leading to abnormal haemoglobin S (HbS). This results in red blood cells assuming a sickle shape under low oxygen conditions, causing vessel blockages. It leads to complications such as vaso-occlusive crises, chronic haemolytic anaemia, and tissue damage, posing a significant public health challenge.1

The clinical spectrum of sickle cell disease includes several forms: Sickle Cell Anemia (HbSS), Sickle Haemoglobin C Disease (HbSC), and Haemoglobin S Beta-Thalassemia Disease (HbSβThal). These conditions manifest when an individual inherits sickle cell traits from both parents, leading to the production of mutant haemoglobin that disrupts normal blood flow and causes premature destruction of red blood cells.2

In addition to the direct effects of sickling, SCD is associated with a variety of systemic complications. These include bone disorders such as osteopenia and osteoporosis, which are associated with increased risk of fractures, vertebral collapse and bone pain.4 The underlying pathophysiology involves increased erythropoiesis, hypoxia due to vessel obstruction, and consequent tissue injury, which adversely affects bone health.3

Electrolyte imbalances and disturbances in bone metabolism are frequently observed in SCD patients. Reports from various studies indicate anomalies in serum levels of calcium, magnesium, and phosphate. For example, there is one study that documented hypocalcaemia and hyperphosphatemia in Nigerian children with SCD, while in another study, low magnesium levels and a high calcium-to-magnesium ratio in adults with SCD patients has been observed. Other research has highlighted the persistent deficiency of magnesium and calcium, which are crucial for maintaining bone integrity and overall health.8,9,10

Magnesium plays a critical role in numerous physiological processes including bone health. It contributes to electrolyte balance, muscle function, energy metabolism, and cell division. Magnesium deficiency has been associated with a range of bone disorders, including osteoporosis, due to its effects on bone remodeling and calcium homeostasis.11,12 Similarly, calcium and phosphorus are essential for skeletal integrity and various physiological functions such as muscle contraction and neuronal excitation. Disruptions in the homeostasis of these minerals can lead to significant bone-related issues.13

In the context of sickle cell disease, disruptions in calcium and phosphorus levels, combined with the effects of repeated vaso-occlusive crises and bone infarcts, contribute to the development of conditions such as avascular necrosis and degenerative arthritis.14 The oxidative stress associated with SCD further complicates the disease. Sickle cell patients experience increased oxidative stress due to heightened production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduced antioxidant defences. This imbalance leads to oxidative damage, which exacerbates haemolysis and contributes to disease complications.15,16 Antioxidant vitamins such as vitamins A, C and E play a crucial role in neutralizing ROS and protecting cells from oxidative damage. Studies have shown that SCD patients have decreased levels of these antioxidants, which further emphasizes the need for monitoring and potentially supplementing antioxidant vitamins as part of the disease management strategy.17

Nigeria, with its high prevalence of sickle cell disease, presents a critical context for this research. With an estimated 24% prevalence of sickle cell trait and 100,000 new SCD births annually, understanding and addressing the nutritional and metabolic imbalances in these patients is vital.18 Evaluating the serum levels of magnesium, calcium, phosphate, and antioxidant vitamins can provide valuable insights into the management of SCD and help in mitigating some of the disease's severe complications.

Therefore, sickle cell disease represents a complex interplay of genetic, metabolic, and oxidative factors that significantly impact patient health. This research will focus on the serum concentrations of key electrolytes (magnesium, calcium, and phosphate) and antioxidant vitamins (Vitamin A, C and E) in sickle cell patients in Kano State, aiming to better understand their role in disease progression and management. By examining these parameters, the study seeks to contribute to improved clinical strategies for managing sickle cell disease and enhancing patient outcomes.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Equipment

The equipment that was used include; spectrophotometer (UV-2100 PC), micropipette, gel activator vacutainer tube, centrifuge (80-1), test-tubes, plain tubes.

Study area

The study was conducted in the outpatient clinics of Murtala Muhammed Specialist Hospital and Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital, both tertiary healthcare facilities in Kano State, Nigeria. Kano State, located in the Northwest geopolitical region, covers 20,760 square kilometers and has a population of 9.4 million, according to the 2006 census.19,20,21 The state borders Katsina, Jigawa, Bauchi, and Kaduna.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical clearance to conduct the research was obtained from the Kano State Ministry of Health. The participant consent was obtained prior to the administration of questionnaires. All study was carried out in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.22

Study Population

The study participants were divided into three groups comprising sickle cell patient with crisis (group 1) and sickle cell patient without crisis (group 2) and healthy control subjects (group 3) both attending Murtala Muhammed Specialist Hospital, and Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital.

Bio-data and Informed Consent

Socio-demographic data and medical history were collected using structured interviews. Participants at the sickle cell clinic were informed about the study, and consent was obtained verbally and in writing.

Inclusion Criteria

  • Tested and confirmed Sickle cell patients with crisis (clinically on pain, fever, vasculitis, multi organ infections and low Hb with high TWBCs) who consent to participate.
  • Sickle cell patients without crisis who consented to participate.
  • Healthy aged matched control subjects

Exclusion Criteria

The study excluded SCD patients with chronic renal failure, hyper-parathyroidism and stroke

Research Design

The study is a hospital-based prospective comparative and cross-sectional which lasted for four months (January to April, 2024).

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was obtained using the formula for the calculation of minimum sample size used in a study involving human subjects/patients, which is:

n = z²pq / d²

p = prevalence of sickle cell disease = 5.00% = 0.05.24 Total samples = 80 samples, Test = 60 samples (Patients with crisis = 30 samples, Patients without crisis = 30 samples) Control = 20 samples.

Sample Collection, Preservation and Processing

About 5ml of venous blood sample was collected aseptically from the ante-cubital fossa of each participant using sterile syringe with the vacutainer tube containing get and clot activator (gel tube). The blood samples were allowed to clot and then centrifuged at 350rpm for 5minutes. The separated serum was then transferred into pre labeled Serum container and stored at -20°C until ready for assay.

Biochemical Analysis of the Samples

Measurement of Serum Calcium: This was achieved by colorimetric method.25 Principle: In alkaline solution, oresolphthalein complexone (CPC) forms a purple complex with calcium. Interference with magnesium ions is reduced by the addition of 8-hydroxyquinoline. The colour intensity of the complex formed is measured and is directly proportional to the calcium concentration in the sample.25 Reagent: propanol (Buffer), O-cresolphthalein complexone and 8-hydroxyquinoline.25

Measurement of Serum Magnesium: This was achieved by Metallochromic dye Xylidyl Blue was used.26 Principle: Serum magnesium ions react with Xylidyl Blue in an alkaline solution to produce a red complex that is measured spectrophotometrically. The intensity of color produced is directly proportional to magnesium concentration. Calcium interference is virtually eliminated by use of EGTA and a surfactant system is included to remove protein interference.26 Reagent: When combined the reagent contains: xylidyl blue 0.1mM, EGTA 0.13mM, DMSO 1.4M, Buffer, surfactant, non-reactive stabilizers including potassium cyanide at 0.02% w/v.26

Measurement of Serum Phosphate: Serum phosphate was measured using colorimetric method.27 Principle: The measurement of inorganic phosphorus in serum is usually accomplished by forming a phosphomolybdate complex and in turn reducing it to a molybdenum blue color complex.27 Reagent: Sulfuric acid Ferrous ammonium sulfate Ferrous nitrate Ammonium, Sulphuric acid, Ammonium molybdate and Phosphorous standard.27

Measurement of Serum Vitamin A: Serum vitamin A was estimated by Spectrophotometrically.28

Measurement of Serum Vitamin C: Serum vitamin C was estimated Spectrophotometrically.28

Estimation of Serum Vitamin E: Serum vitamin E concentration was estimated Spectrophotometrically.28

Statistical Analysis

The data were stored in a Microsoft excel 2016 and then analyzed using statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS) for windows version 23.0. The data were expressed as Mean ± Standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance between means were estimated using ANOVA when appropriate. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was used to measure the strength of the association between the variables. The p<0.05 was considered to be significant.

Comparison of the Biochemical Parameters of SCD Patients with Crisis and SCD Patient on Steady Health, as well as Healthy Control Subjects

ANOVA analysis revealed significant variations in serum electrolyte and antioxidant levels among sickle cell patients in crisis, those in steady health, and healthy controls. Sickle cell patients in crisis had notably lower serum calcium levels (1.20±0.30 mmol/L) compared to steady-state patients (1.97±0.16 mmol/L) and healthy controls (2.17±0.18 mmol/L) (p = 0.035). Additionally, serum phosphate levels were significantly elevated during crises (2.19±1.50 mmol/L) compared to steady health and controls (p = 0.016), while serum magnesium levels were markedly reduced (0.48±0.21 mmol/L) during crises. Antioxidant vitamins A and E were significantly lower in sickle cell patients, with consistent Vitamin C levels in crisis and steady-state groups, but significantly decreased in comparison to controls.

DISCUSSION

Electrolyte imbalances are prevalent in sickle cell disease (SCD) and pose significant risks to patient health. Essential electrolytes like calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus, which play vital roles in physiological functions, often show abnormal levels in SCD patients. These disturbances can exacerbate the complications associated with the disease, particularly during a sickle cell crisis- a condition marked by acute pain and tissue ischemia due to the blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red blood cells.29

The current study investigated serum calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus levels as well as antioxidants vitamins (Vitamin A, C, and E) in SCD patients during crises, those in steady states, and healthy controls. The results revealed notable differences in these electrolytes across the groups. Specifically, during a sickle cell crisis, patients exhibited lower serum calcium levels, while magnesium and phosphorus levels were elevated compared to both steady-state SCD patients and healthy individuals.

In SCD, calcium disturbances are closely linked to bone disorders such as osteoporosis and osteopenia, which are more common due to chronic hemolysis, inflammation, and impaired bone remodeling.5,6 The study found that serum calcium levels were significantly lower during crises compared to non-crisis patients and healthy controls. This decrease in calcium levels may result from an inflammatory response during crises, leading to increased calcium utilization by immune cells, dehydration, and reduced calcium absorption.

Hypocalcemia have been frequently observed in individuals with sickle cell anemia (SCA), particularly during vaso-occlusive crises (VOC). Research indicates that hypocalcemia may be associated with reduced dietary intake in children with SCA, potentially contributing to the increased frequency of VOC.30 Hypocalcemia during VOC in SCA children prolongs QTc interval, increasing risk of ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest.31,32 The findings of this study are consistent with previous research,6,9,33 which also reported lower serum calcium levels in SCD patients compared to control.

In this study we found low serum magnesium levels in SCD patients on crisis and steady-health compared to healthy controls. Reports from previous studies on the level of magnesium in sickle cell patients have been variable, ranging from low to normal.5,6,10 Some studies have shown that magnesium levels are low in sickle cell anemia5,10 while others reported normal levels.6

Despite normal serum magnesium levels, decreased RBC magnesium concentrations have been documented in individuals with sickle cell disease (SCD), potentially due to increased activity of the Na/Mg exchanges in magnesium-loaded sickle erythrocytes.34 Magnesium plays a crucial role in SCD beyond its effect on RBC dehydration, potentially influencing vascular tone through nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation and calcium inhibition in smooth muscle. Additionally, magnesium's ability to prolong clotting time might contribute to a reduction in vaso-occlusion.35

Phosphorus plays a key role in cellular energy metabolism, bone mineralization, and nucleic acid synthesis. The study observed elevated serum phosphorus levels during crises compared to steady-state patients and healthy controls. Elevated phosphorus levels observed during crises may result from increased cell turnover and hemolysis, leading to the release of intracellular phosphate into the bloodstream. Additionally, the heightened metabolic demands of the inflammatory response during a crisis could enhance phosphate mobilization to support energy metabolism and cellular repair.13

This study is in consistent with one study,38 that reported significantly higher serum phosphate in the sickle SCD patients compared to control. They related their findings to increased tubular reabsorption of phosphate in these patients and also to the increased urinary sodium clearance. Similarly, another study has also reported significantly higher serum phosphorus in Nigerian SCD children than in control.6 Contrary to our findings, some studies,36,37 reported lower serum phosphate in children with sickle disease which they linked it to their renal phosphate reabsorption as a result of increased parathyroid hormone.
